# TONE

The governing doc for how an activity *sounds* — the one that rules them all. Every line of copy
is doing one of three jobs, and the voice shifts depending on which, over **one shared voice**
underneath. Get the **surface's job** right first; then apply the shared voice.

| Surface | Its job | Voice | Person | Deep guide |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Intro** | orient + **motivate** — make them want to start | energetic, pulls you in | "we" / "us" | §Intro below |
| **Prompt** (middle steps) | **invite self-disclosure** — make it safe to put a piece of yourself down | open, gentle, spacious | "you" | §Prompt below |
| **Closing** (reflection) | **witness** — help them notice what they noticed | soft, reflective | "you" | §Closing below |
| **Mirror** (scored or selected read-back) | **show them what they just said** — name the experience without diagnosing | warm, plain, vivid-quantified | "you" | [`SCORED_REFLECTIONS.md`](./SCORED_REFLECTIONS.md) |

The intro and the prompt do nearly *opposite* jobs. An intro leans in and pulls; a prompt steps
back and makes room. Don't write them in the same voice.

**Related docs:** [`OPEN-ENDED-QUESTIONS.md`](./OPEN-ENDED-QUESTIONS.md) (the full question
mechanics) · [`WHOLE-ASS-PARAGRAPH.md`](./WHOLE-ASS-PARAGRAPH.md) (sourcing content from clinical
material + the exhaustive voice do/don't list) ·
[`SCORED_REFLECTIONS.md`](./SCORED_REFLECTIONS.md) (the per-item variation arrays the

## The shared voice (under all three surfaces)

- **Warm-supportive** — a friend who knows a lot about feelings, not a clinician with a clipboard.
- **Vivid and concrete** over abstract. Specific small moments, not big claims.
- **Warm is not flat.** Copy should have forward motion; gentle is not the same as limp.
- **Picture the reader.** The same line reads differently to a well person and to a struggling
  one. Read each prompt as if the most fragile plausible user is opening it — soften imperatives
  that would land as accusatory, lead validation before challenge.
- **For heavy material, honor before redirect.** Grief, despair, severe shame, suicidality-adjacent
  topics: name the weight first; only then move toward what the activity does with it.
- **Human action, never the UI.** "Keep track of how many times it slips in" — never "tap the
  counter." No interface nouns (counter, slider, field, button, screen, widget) in body copy.
- **Spoken cadence.** Short sentences, gentle fragments, plain words ("dwell on", not "host").
  Read it aloud; if it sounds like a form, rewrite it. Emphasis is italics — never ALL CAPS.
- **Permission-granting**, observational not prescriptive. No commands, no "should", no promises
  ("you'll feel better"), no judgment verbs (drift, default, fall into), no empty reassurance.

The exhaustive do/don't list lives in [`WHOLE-ASS-PARAGRAPH.md`](./WHOLE-ASS-PARAGRAPH.md) §5; the
above is the short form that governs every surface.

## Transitions — keeping pace between steps

An activity should read as one continuous thing, not a stack of disconnected screens. When the
**format or mode shifts** from one step to the next — a rating step into a writing step, worked
examples into your-own-turn, naming a thing into mapping it, gathering into deciding — a short
**bridge** wraps up what just happened and leads into what's next. That hand-off is what keeps the
pace; without it, each step lands cold.

- **Only where it's earned.** Not every step needs one. A transition belongs where the *mode*
  changes or a section closes. Steps that simply continue the same kind of work flow on their own —
  forcing "Now that you've done X, let's do Y" onto every step reads robotic.
- **Short.** A clause or a sentence, woven into the receiving step's opening prose (after the
  carry-over recap) — or as a closing line on the step you're leaving. Never a paragraph.
- **Forward-looking and warm**, and varied: *"You've marked where things tend to land — now let's
  put a few real moments to it."* · *"That's the shape of it; here's where you might lean in."* ·
  *"With those named, one quiet question is left."*
- It's the same instinct as a good intro's third beat (*how it turns to your own life*) — applied
  at every seam where the activity changes gears.
- **The difficulty curve is part of the pacing too.** For gradient activities (a ladder, a graded
  exposure, a behavioral activation), each step should be reachable from where the user currently
  stands. Start small enough that the most isolated/stuck reader can take the first move.
- **If they named things, treat each thing.** When an earlier step has the user pick or name a
  list (three activities, two options, four roles), the activity walks each one in turn — not a
  single bulk summary that collapses them. They did the work of individuating; honor it.
- **Carry-over is part of the read, not just a schema rule.** Show only what the user actually
  picked, in their own words, with a plain-language label that names what they gave you ("the
  belief you brought" — not "belief"). A raw dump of every prior bind reads as noise; a curated
  mirror reads as care. R5 in [`_SCHEMA.md`](./activities/v2/_SCHEMA.md) is the mechanism; how
  it lands is the tone.

## Surface — Intros: orient and motivate

The intro's job is to make someone *want* to begin. Our intros are usually detailed enough; what
they miss is **orientation + a reason to care**. Lead with both.

### The bar (the "one-liner test")

> After the **first few sentences**, a newcomer should be able to say both **"I'm about to ___"**
> (the thing + the shape of the work) **and "…because ___"** (the tension it works on).

Example (Hot cross bun): *"I'm about to take one recent situation and break my reaction into four
corners — thoughts, feelings, body, behaviour — and see how they pull on each other, because in
the moment I only ever catch one or two of them."*

Clear the bar on **energy**, not just presence. The standard is the *white bear* / *DOTS* intro —
copy that grabs you — not a competent paragraph that merely contains a why. **Assume almost every
intro can be sharper, and revise it** (leaving one untouched should be the exception).

### Derive the core from the actual steps (don't guess from the title)

Read the middle steps and extract four things, then write them as a **three-beat narrative**:

- **WHY / TENSION** — the cost of the habit, what's at stake, the reason to bother.
- **OBJECT** — what they work on (a recent moment, an inner ache, an unwanted thought, a goal).
- **OPERATION + STRUCTURE** — what they do to it and its shape (break into 4 corners; walk an
  acronym; a 60-second experiment then generalize; practice 5 tips against one situation).
- **PAYOFF** — what becomes visible, or what they leave holding.

> **why it matters → what we'll do (plain human terms) → how it turns to your own life**

Vary the opener — *"So why don't we…"*, *"We're going to…"*, *"Let's start by…"* — never the stock
"In this activity, we'll…".

### Intro rules

- **Make the why *vivid*, not abstract.** A concrete image beats a category. DOTS went from
  "avoidance has costs" → *"the bill comes later, in a currency we didn't mean to spend — the
  things we step around run our days from the back seat."*
- **Give the rationale; withhold only the firsthand result.** Say *why* the habit is worth
  examining; only hold back the thing the user is meant to *feel for themselves* (white bear:
  that the bear shows up *more*). Being coy about the why reads as withholding the point.
- **Logistics are secondary — trim hard.** Time estimates and "nothing to get right" boilerplate
  are optional; never let them crowd the core. Don't announce the activity is an activity
  ("This is a small experiment in…") — name the stakes and begin.
- **Walk-with, don't describe-at.** An intro reads as if you're standing next to them, narrating
  what you're both about to do — never as if you're describing a product feature. Test: if you can
  lift the intro and drop it into a marketing page, it's a brochure, not a guide. *"This activity
  will help you identify…"* is sideways; *"So why don't we pull out just one and walk it through
  five steps…"* is forward.
- **The acronym/framework template.** Activities built on a named framework share a shape:
  *scenario hook + why → the acronym as a bulleted list → a tight "here's how it'll go" → pre_mood.*
  (FEAR → DARE and DOTS are the matched pair.)
- **Component order:** opening hook → core (woven in or its own short paragraph) → any framing
  bulleted-list → trimmed logistics → `pre_mood`. Never move or alter the `pre_mood` widget.

### The white bear — the worked example

> Most of us have a thought or two we'd rather not dwell on… The instinct is to push it away,
> **but that can do more harm than good — giving the thought power and acting as a constant
> distraction.**
>
> **So why don't we** take something harmless, like a white bear. And try pushing it away, just
> for sixty seconds. **Keep track of how many times it slips in.**
>
> Then we can take whatever that shows you and turn toward the thoughts you'd actually rather not have.

It (1) states the why, (2) describes the *human action* — "keep track," not "tap the counter",
(3) uses spoken, invitational cadence, and (4) drops the logistics paragraph entirely.

## Surface — Prompts: the personal hinge

A prompt is where someone puts a piece of themselves on the page — the most intimate line in the
activity, the personal connection to their psyche that makes it safe to unload a little of what
they're carrying. Its job is **invitation, not instruction.** If a prompt reads like a form field
or an assignment, the person answers thinly; if it reads like a warm, specific question from someone
who gets it, they tell you something true. An intro **pulls**; a prompt **opens**.

**Write prompts as open questions, in second person — never labels, commands, or form-fills.**

> ❌ "Describe a situation where you acted passively."
> ✅ "Has there been a feeling, lately, of saying yes when something inside meant no?"
>
> ❌ "List your distraction behaviors."
> ✅ "When the feeling shows up, where does your attention tend to go instead?"

### Prompt rules

- **Question form, second person.** If it doesn't end in a question mark, ask whether it should.
- **Vary the entry point across sibling prompts** — a feeling, a relationship, an image, a recall,
  an observation. Three prompts that all open "Think of a time you…" blur together no matter how
  different the content.
- **One thing per question.** Don't pre-list the emotional territory ("hurt, fear, exhaustion…") —
  that does the reader's work and narrows what they can bring. Ask the one thing; let them find
  their own word.
- **For framework prompts (the C's, the P's, the corners, the rings): ask, don't define.** *"Where
  do you feel like things are out of your hands lately?"* — not *"Control is the sense of agency
  over your circumstances. Rate yours."* The framework belongs in the intro; by the time the user
  is at the prompt they're not here for definitions. The category label can sit above the question
  as a heading; the question itself is always a question.
- **Match length to weight.** A harder, more exposed question can afford to be *shorter* — brevity
  makes space. When every prompt is the same length, the emotional difference flattens.
- **Weave the escape hatch in, don't bolt it on.** "Has there been…" already invites "no / sort of."
  Never frame the alternative as "skip" or "leave blank" — the person is participating either way.
- **No trait-attribution / self-diagnostic asks.** Avoid "what this says about you" or "how this
  pattern shows up in your relationship with X." They didn't sign up to diagnose themselves.
- **Offer a side door when the main path may not apply.** If the default path assumes access the
  user may not have (people to reach out to, a partner to talk to, a doctor to call), include a
  brief aside naming an alternative they can take alone (a volunteer setting, a written letter, a
  public space). Phrase it as an offer, not a fallback: *"And if there isn't a specific person —
  there's also…"*
- **The split, both directions.** The prompt carries the question and any framing the user needs
  to answer. The placeholder carries the example (`e.g. …`) — *only* the example. If your
  placeholder is explaining what the question is really asking, hinting at categories, or doing
  emotional reassurance, that work belongs in the prompt; pull it back up. And remember the
  placeholder is still tone — it's the example the user will pattern their answer on. A clinical
  example invites clinical answers; a soft, lived-in example invites lived-in answers. See
  [`EXAMPLES.md`](./EXAMPLES.md).
- **Exception — guided practice.** Body scans, breath meditations, paced sequences and other
  guided practices use mild imperative voice as a feature, not a violation ("begin at your feet",
  "follow the circle"). The no-commands rule applies to instruction-as-evaluation, not to the
  gentle leading voice of a guided practice. Keep imperatives short, soft, and paced; never stack
  three commands without a breath.

**Why questions, not statements:** the point of a prompt is self-contact — noticing and saying
something true to yourself. "Describe… / List… / Identify…" puts the user in report-mode, performing
for an evaluator; a question invites them into their own experience. The full rule set with extended
examples is in [`OPEN-ENDED-QUESTIONS.md`](./OPEN-ENDED-QUESTIONS.md).

## Surface — Closings: witness

The closing reflection (the timed `JournalStep`, with `ReflectionFraming` as its header) helps the
person notice what *they* noticed — it does not quiz them on the activity's content.

- **Three soft questions:** *attention* (what did you notice?) → *meaning* (what might it suggest?)
  → *forward intention* (what could you try, gently?).
- **Witness, don't report.** "What did you learn about yourself?" not "Which trap scored highest?"
- **Honor what they did, by name.** A closing that ends an activity without acknowledging what the
  user just put on the page reads as a dump. Name the work — *"You looked at the belief from four
  angles"* / *"You named three small moves"* — then witness. The acknowledgement is the bridge
  between effort and meaning.
- **The terminal SummaryOutputCard is part of this surface.** Its `closingPromptText` is the last
  thing the user reads. Treat it as a closing line, not a system message: warm, specific to what
  they did, never a generic "Saved!" or "Activity complete."
- The `ReflectionFraming` header ("Give yourself a little space…") sits directly over the box.

## Applying this — and where review lives

- **No coasting in the middle.** Tone is the whole activity's job, not the intro's. If the intro
  lands and the middle steps read like a worksheet, the tone pass isn't done. Every prompt, every
  transition, every closing line gets the same revision energy the intro gets. The most common
  failure mode is a great intro followed by mechanical bodies; assume that's your activity until
  you've reread every step aloud.
- **Default to revising.** A "technically fine but flat" surface is not passing. Most existing copy
  can be sharper; bias toward rewriting over leaving alone.
- **One pass per surface.** Editing an intro is a different job from editing prompts. (Engagement
  scaffolding — the concrete example placeholders — is a separate middle-step concern; flag it,
  don't fold it into a tone pass.)
- **Tone review** is per-activity in `activities/v2/audits/<slug>.md` (each has a `## Tone findings`
  section), tracked by `ACTIVITY_REVIEW_PROGRESS.md` and indexed by
  `activities/v2/audits/CANONICAL_PROTOTYPE_DISCREPANCIES.md`. The schema's R1
  ("Tone of voice for prompts and reflections") is the rule authors read at generation time.

### Running a tone pass with an agent

When fixing one surface across many activities, spawn agents that edit in place and return a terse
report — then validate JSON and reseed **once** at the end (agents do not reseed). Report format:

```
<activityId> — <REVISED | ALREADY_MEETS_BAR>
  surface: <intro | prompt | closing>
  change: <none | the before→after of the edited copy>
```
